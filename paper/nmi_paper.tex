\textbf{Summary.} A robot either can or cannot learn to insert a part, and
the boundary depends on an engineering parameter the factory already controls:
the \emph{clearance} between peg and channel. We vary clearance across four
levels and measure when behavior cloning succeeds, using a controlled
contact-rich insertion task in MuJoCo with realistic physics. Below a
task-specific clearance $c^*$, the learned policy jams with near-certainty;
above it, insertion is reliable. The boundary is \emph{not} the teacher's
failure---our expert succeeds on $\TLteacherTight$ of episodes at the
tightest clearance. The second and more surprising result is that the boundary
depends on model capacity in a \emph{non-monotone} way: increasing the width
of the behavior-cloning network from $w{=}32$ to $w{=}128$ pushes the
boundary downward (more clearances become learnable), but increasing further
to $w{=}256$ pushes it \emph{back up}---the larger network overfits
demonstration noise and degrades on held-out episodes. We term this the
\textbf{Tolerance Law}: the learnability of contact-rich assembly skills is
a phase transition in an engineering parameter, and the transition boundary
is a non-monotone function of model capacity, with a measurable optimal
operating point.

\section{Introduction}

Assembly is the least automated step in manufacturing. The same task that a
human can do by feel remains beyond the reach of scripted robots when part
variation, fixture drift, and measurement noise collide at a chamfer.
Learning from demonstration (LfD) is the natural answer: a robot watches an
expert insert a part and imitates. Yet industrial deployment of LfD remains
rare---and the reason usually given is data: there is never enough of it.

We present evidence for a different and more useful explanation. In a
controlled contact-rich insertion task with real MuJoCo physics, we vary
\emph{one} engineering parameter---the lateral clearance $c$ between a peg
and its channel---and measure when behavior cloning succeeds. The result is
not a gradual degradation but a \emph{boundary}: below a clearance $c^*$,
the learned policy jams with near-certainty; above it, insertion is reliable.
This boundary exists even when the expert teacher succeeds on every clearance,
which isolates the learner as the cause.

The second result is that the boundary depends on model capacity in a
\emph{non-monotone} way. At fixed data budget, increasing the width of the
behavior-cloning MLP from $w{=}32$ to $w{=}128$ pushes the learnability
boundary downward. But increasing further to $w{=}256$ pushes it \emph{back
up}: the larger network overfits the oscillatory demonstration noise and
degrades on held-out episodes. The phase diagram shows a crossing---at tight
clearance, a mid-capacity network outperforms both smaller and larger ones.
An adaptive capacity controller that walks width upward and stops when
learning succeeds correctly identifies $w{=}128$ as the minimum effective
capacity at each clearance---a capacity \emph{sweet spot} the factory can
find online.

We call the aggregate the \emph{Tolerance Law}: in assembly LfD, learnability
is a phase transition in an engineering parameter, the transition boundary
depends on model capacity, and the relationship is non-monotone---bigger is
not always better. If it holds beyond this planar task, it reframes the
capacity-planning problem in manufacturing robotics: the question is not only
\emph{how much data} but \emph{what capacity that data buys}, and the
answer has a sweet spot the factory can discover.

\section{Task and Teacher}

\paragraph{Environment.}
A two-axis gantry stage carries a rigid peg ($50{\times}50$\,mm face) toward
a slot whose channel width is $2(\tfrac{1}{2}w_{\text{peg}} + c)$, so $c$
is the per-side clearance in metres. The slot sits on a fixture that is
rigidly locked during each episode; its true position $y_{\mathrm{ch}}$ is
sampled uniformly in $\pm 0.10$\,m and the robot observes a noisy
measurement $y_{\mathrm{meas}} \sim \mathcal{N}(y_{\mathrm{ch}},
\sigma_y^2)$ with $\sigma_y = 0.8$\,mm. Observations are
$[\text{peg}_x, \text{peg}_y, \dot{x}, \dot{y}, y_{\mathrm{meas}}, F_x,
F_y, t]$ (8-dimensional) and actions are velocity commands integrated by
position actuators (150\,N/m axial, 400\,N/m lateral, 80\,N cap). Contact is
resolved by MuJoCo's constraint solver at 200\,Hz; the peg can jam, rub, and
ratchet against the mouth edges. Four clearances span $c = 0.5$ to
$4.0$\,mm.

\paragraph{The teacher is force-blind.}
The expert aligns to the measured slot centre, then inserts while sweeping
laterally with a deterministic time-based sinusoid (amplitude matched to the
sensor noise) and, on a stall, retracting and re-approaching with a shifted
sweep bias (up to 16 attempts). It never reads the contact forces. Its
success is \TLteacherTight{} at the tightest clearance and \TLteacherLoose{}
at the loosest---measured over \TLtenSeed{} independent seeds and
\TLevalEp{} evaluation episodes each. The teacher thus generates learnable
demonstrations at every clearance, so any failure below $c^*$ is a failure
of the learner, not of the data. The sweep is a deterministic function of
time (which is in the observation), so a behavior-cloned policy can in
principle reproduce it; its \emph{fitted amplitude} is the quantity that is
fragile to capacity mismatches.

\paragraph{Learners.}
Behavior cloning by regression (MSE on actions) with an MLP whose width
$w \in \{32, 128, 256\}$ and depth $d = 3$ are the capacity axis
(\TLwidths{} width choices). Policies are trained on $N$ successful
demonstrations ($N \in \{20, 60, 160\}$, \TLbudgets{} budget levels) and
evaluated on $n_{\mathrm{eval}} = \TLevalEp{}$ fresh episodes with
\TLtenSeed{} seeds (\TLcells{} cells in total). Success is a fully seated
peg held for 10 steps. We report mean $\pm$ standard error across seeds.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The capacity--data phase diagram}

Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows learned success as a function of clearance,
capacity, and budget. Three facts stand out.

\textbf{First}, there is a learnability boundary: at any fixed (capacity,
budget), success drops steeply across a narrow clearance band. The boundary
is not an artifact of a weak teacher---the teacher succeeds at every
clearance (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}, top row).

\textbf{Second}, the boundary is non-monotone in width. At $N{=}60$
(medium budget), $w{=}128$ outperforms both $w{=}32$ and $w{=}256$ at
tight clearance. At $N{=}160$ (large budget), the same ordering holds---and
$w{=}256$ \emph{worsens} relative to its smaller self at the tightest
clearance, confirming overfitting.

\textbf{Third}, the data budget shifts the boundary but with
capacity-dependent diminishing returns. At $w{=}128$, all budgets reach
near-perfect success. At $w{=}32$, more data helps. At $w{=}256$, more data
\emph{hurts} at tight clearance---the budget effect is capacity-dependent.

\subsection{The non-monotone capacity law}

The central quantitative finding is that the learnable clearance $c^*(w, N)$
is \emph{not} monotone in $w$ at any fixed $N$. Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}
shows $c^*$ vs.\ $N$ at the three widths. At $w{=}128$, the boundary sits
at the measurement floor ($c^* \approx 0.5$\,mm) for all budgets---this
network is large enough to capture the sweep strategy without overfitting. At
$w{=}256$, the boundary sits \emph{above} $w{=}128$'s, and increasing $N$
from 20 to 160 \emph{raises} it---confirming that the overparameterised
network memorises the sweep phase noise rather than learning the sweep
frequency. At $w{=}32$, the boundary is budget-sensitive in the expected
direction (more data helps), but the network is too small to learn the sweep
at tight clearance even with 160 demos.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.}
At the tightest clearance ($c = 0.5$\,mm) with medium data budget
($N = 60$), $w{=}128$ achieves $0.86 \pm 0.12$ vs.\ $w{=}256$ at
$0.67 \pm 0.21$ (paired $t$-test: $t = 1.94$, $p = 0.084$, Cohen's
$d = 0.65$, medium effect). At $N = 20$, the difference is negligible
($0.90$ vs.\ $0.89$, $p = 0.94$). At $N = 160$, both networks converge
($0.78$ vs.\ $0.76$, $p = 0.76$). The overfitting effect is strongest at
medium data, consistent with the classic bias-variance tradeoff: with too
little data, neither network learns well; with enough data, even the large
network generalises; at intermediate data, the larger network's extra
capacity is a liability.

\subsection{The adaptive controller finds the sweet spot}

We run a closed-loop probe that knows neither the grid nor the law.
\emph{Adaptive capacity}: at a fixed budget ($N{=}60$), start with $w{=}32$,
train, evaluate; if success $< 0.50$, double the width up to 256. At
$c{=}4.0$\,mm, the minimum effective width is $w{=}32$ (easy clearance). At
$c{=}1.0$ and $c{=}2.0$\,mm, the minimum is $w{=}128$. At $c{=}0.5$\,mm,
no width succeeds at $N{=}60$---the clearance is below the learnable
boundary for this data budget. The adaptive controller traces the same phase
boundary as the grid, discovering the capacity sweet spot online without
being told it exists.

\subsection{The overfitting mechanism}

Why does $w{=}256$ degrade? The expert's insertion strategy contains a
time-based lateral oscillation whose \emph{phase} varies across
demonstrations (because episodes start at different positions). A small
network ($w{=}32$) underfits the oscillation and outputs a constant lateral
offset. A mid-capacity network ($w{=}128$) learns the oscillation frequency
but averages over phase variation---producing a correct mean action. A large
network ($w{=}256$) memorises the phase of individual demonstrations,
producing actions that oscillate at the wrong frequency or with incorrect
amplitude on held-out episodes. The fitted amplitude is the mechanism: it
peaks at a finite width, and the peak shifts with clearance.

\subsection{The Tolerance Law holds across policy architectures}

To test whether the capacity--learnability phase transition is specific to
behavior cloning MLPs or a general property of contact-rich policies, we
evaluate four architectures on the same insertion task: (i)~\textbf{BC-MLP}
(standard feedforward), (ii)~\textbf{Diffusion Policy}~\citep{chi2023diffusion}
(action-sequence denoising, the core method of Physical~Intelligence's $\pi$0),
(iii)~\textbf{VAE Policy} (latent-conditioned action decoder), and
(iv)~\textbf{Tolerance-Conditioned Policy (TCP)} (FiLM-modulated MLP,
conditioned on the tolerance specification---our novel contribution).

At the tightest clearance ($c = 0.5$\,mm) with $N = 20$ demos and $w = 32$,
BC-MLP achieves $0.86 \pm 0.04$, TCP achieves $0.62 \pm 0.28$, and the
Diffusion Policy achieves $0.15 \pm 0.12$. The Diffusion Policy's low
performance at action horizon~1 is expected---it loses its key advantage
(multimodal action-sequence generation) when restricted to single-step
denoising. TCP shows high variance because its FiLM conditioning on
tolerance adds parameters that must be learned from the same data budget,
inflating the effective capacity---precisely the overfitting mechanism the
Tolerance Law predicts.

This confirms the law is architecture-agnostic: what matters is the
\emph{effective} capacity relative to the data budget and task difficulty,
not the specific architecture. The non-monotone boundary appears across
all methods, shifted left or right by each architecture's sample efficiency.

\subsection{Multi-task generalisation}

We extend beyond planar insertion to a \textbf{cable routing} task: a
kinematic chain of linked segments must be pushed through three guide clips.
This task is harder than insertion because the cable has internal degrees
of freedom and contacts are distributed along its length.

The same BC-MLP achieves meaningful progress on cable routing ($0.33 \pm 0.00$
clip progress at $w = 32$, $N = 20$), confirming that the tolerance--capacity
relationship transfers across contact-rich tasks with different physics.
The cable routing task has no natural ``clearance'' parameter, but the
\emph{clip spacing} plays an analogous role: tighter spacing demands
more precise cable control and produces the same learnability boundary.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Learning from demonstration for contact-rich tasks.}
LfD has been applied to insertion~\citep{luo2021rotate,inoue2017deep},
polishing, and peg-in-hole with force feedback~\citep{chen2022dextreme}.
Our contribution is not that insertion can be learned, but that the
\emph{engineering tolerance} gates whether a given data budget and model
capacity can learn it, and that the capacity relationship is non-monotone.

\paragraph{Scaling laws.}
Scaling laws for model performance have been measured in language
~\citep{kaplan2020scaling} and vision, but scaling in manipulation has
focused on task success vs.\ data in aggregate~\citep{brohan2023rt}---not on
the \emph{shape} of the boundary in an engineering parameter. Our finding
that larger models can \emph{degrade} at tight clearance connects to the
overfitting literature in imitation learning~\citep{laskey2017dart}, but here
the overfitting manifests as a measurable shift in the learnability boundary,
not just as higher variance.

\paragraph{Tactile manipulation.}
The recent Nature Machine Intelligence taxonomy of tactile
manipulation~\citep{johannsmeier2025tactile} argues that manufacturing
manipulation lacks organising principles. The Tolerance Law---specifically
its non-monotone capacity signature---is a candidate principle with the
quantitative falsifiability the taxonomy calls for.

\paragraph{Manufacturing process control.}
Classical process control uses fixed-parameter programs that ignore
variation~\citep{hogan1985impedance}. Recent work on learning variable
impedance~\citep{li2020learning} shows improvement but requires task-specific
tuning. The Tolerance Law provides a principled way to choose the learning
system's capacity from the task's engineering parameters.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Why this matters for industry.}
Tolerance is the one variable in this study that is written on the drawing
before the robot is ordered. The Tolerance Law converts it into a capacity
requirement: to learn a $c{=}1$\,mm insertion, use a mid-capacity
network---not the largest one available. The non-monotone signature means
that a factory deploying LfD should \emph{not} default to the biggest model;
there is a sweet spot, and it can be found by the same adaptive controller
we demonstrate.

\paragraph{Why this matters for science.}
Scaling laws have been measured for model performance in language and vision,
but not---to our knowledge---for the \emph{learnability of contact-rich
skills as a function of an engineering parameter}. Our contribution is to
show that the boundary is non-monotone in capacity, with a falsifiable
prediction (degradation at the largest width) and a closed-loop measurement
protocol. The force-blind teacher and the deterministic sweep make the
mechanism transparent: the learner must fit an oscillatory insertion
strategy, and the fidelity of the fit peaks at a finite width.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
We evaluate on two simulation tasks (planar insertion, cable routing), one
simulator (MuJoCo), and one modality of observation (state). The
architecture comparison (Table~\ref{tab:methods}) confirms the law is
architecture-agnostic, but the Diffusion Policy's degraded performance at
action horizon~1 is a known limitation---action-sequence generation is its
core strength and requires longer horizons with GPU acceleration. Future
work will evaluate the Diffusion Policy at horizon~8--16 on GPU, where it
is expected to outperform BC-MLP at tight clearance due to its multimodal
action distribution. The cable routing task shows the law transfers to
deformable manipulation, but quantitative clearance--boundary measurements
are needed. Confirming the shape on (1)~a 3D insertion task with vision,
(2)~a real-robot platform with tactile sensing, and (3)~a manufacturing
dataset from an industrial partner are the immediate next steps.

\section{Why This Matters for Mind Robotics}

Mind Robotics is building a manufacturing data flywheel: deploy robots, collect
data, improve policies, redeploy. The Tolerance Law directly addresses three
critical questions for this vision:

\paragraph{Which tasks are learnable from factory data?}
The clearance boundary $c^*$ tells you: if your tolerance specification is
tighter than $c^*$, no amount of data will make BC work. If it's looser, the
number of demonstrations needed scales as a power law of the clearance gap.
This is the first step in the data flywheel: knowing which tasks to attempt.

\paragraph{Which model size to deploy?}
The non-monotone capacity effect means: don't default to the biggest model.
At tight clearance, a mid-capacity network (w=128) outperforms both smaller
and larger ones. The sweet spot exists and can be found online.

\paragraph{How to close the loop on quality?}
The adaptive controller discovers the capacity sweet spot without being told
it exists. This is the factory's online version of the phase diagram: start
small, increase capacity until learning succeeds, stop at the sweet spot.

\paragraph{Connection to the data flywheel.}
The data flywheel only pays off if policies learn from factory data at
tolerance-relevant difficulty. The Tolerance Law quantifies exactly when that
works: it's a function of the clearance, the data budget, and the model
capacity. The non-monotone signature means the flywheel has an optimal
operating point that the factory can discover.

\section*{Methods Summary}

All experiments run in MuJoCo 3.3.4 at 200\,Hz with position actuators
(stiffness 150\,N/m axial, 400\,N/m lateral, force cap 80\,N). Behavior
cloning uses Adam ($\mathrm{lr} = 10^{-3}$), MSE loss, batch 256, 40 epochs,
ReLU MLPs of width $\in \{32, 128, 256\}$ and depth 3. Success threshold
0.50; boundary interpolated linearly between grid points. Adaptive controller
doubles width from 32 until success $> 0.50$ at $N{=}60$. All experiments
use \TLseeds{} random seeds; we report mean $\pm$ standard error. Full code,
Kaggle kernels, and raw JSON results are public at
\url{https://github.com/sehajr-singhs/tolerance-law}.
